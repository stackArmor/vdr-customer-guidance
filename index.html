02 / What’s changing

Vulnerabilities are scored
in the context of the asset.

The same vulnerability can have different consequences on different assets. Under VDR / VER, remediation deadlines depend on what the affected asset does, who it could affect, and whether the flaw is reachable and likely to be exploited. Your classification metadata supplies the asset context.

ASSET CONTEXT

What could compromise affect?

The data and function an asset supports, its confidentiality / integrity / availability requirements, and whether one or several agencies could be affected.

→
EVALUATION

What is the potential impact?

PAIN evaluates a vulnerability in the context of the affected asset. Scanner severity is one input to the broader evaluation.

→
RESPONSE

What needs action, and when?

PAIN, exploitability, internet reachability, and Certification Class set the response priority and remediation timeframe.

Terms you’ll see.

TSW
stackArmor’s platform that tracks findings, calculates PAIN, and produces VDR / VER reporting.
VDR / VER
FedRAMP Vulnerability Detection and Response / Vulnerability Evaluation and Reporting.
PAIN
Potential Agency Impact Number: a vulnerability’s impact given the affected asset’s requirements and agency scope.
CR / IR / AR
The asset’s confidentiality, integrity, and availability requirements: its security-impact profile.
multiAgency
Whether compromise of the asset could affect one agency or several.
Certification Class
FedRAMP Class A–D, corresponding to your Ready / Low / Moderate / High status.
IRV / NIRV
Internet-reachable / non-internet-reachable vulnerability.
LEV / NLEV
Likely / not likely exploitable vulnerability, based on CISA KEV, EPSS, and exposure.

How TSW meets the eight FedRAMP evaluation factors. Criticality, reachability, exploitability, detectability, prevalence, privilege, proximate vulnerabilities, and known threats each inform impact, exposure, likelihood, or response scope. 05 / Classify your assets

What goes into an
asset’s profile.

Each asset gets an independent confidentiality, integrity, and availability requirement, plus an agency scope. Your mission owners and engineers provide the context; the profile records it consistently.

C

Confidentiality

What information, credentials, or administrative capability could disclosure expose?

CR · LOW / MEDIUM / HIGH
I

Integrity

What trusted data, configuration, identity, or action could an attacker change?

IR · LOW / MEDIUM / HIGH
A

Availability

What would stop working if this asset’s function were unavailable?

AR · LOW / MEDIUM / HIGH

Confirm who could be affected.

The multiAgency setting is an architectural decision: a shared service may have a different scope from a dedicated tenant’s workload.

Single agency
multiAgency: false
Compromise is limited to one agency—for example, dedicated tenant infrastructure with an isolated impact boundary.
Multi-agency
multiAgency: true
Compromise of shared infrastructure could affect multiple agencies. This can increase the applicable PAIN tier.

Review what the skill drafted.

Confirm your Certification Class, any asset-level exceptions, coverage, obvious mismatches, and assumptions flagged for review. Resolve material uncertainty with your stackArmor team.

A profile can be a direct vector such as cr-h_ir-m_ar-l, a three-part decision trace, or a named archetype. The classification guide explains the taxonomy.

!

Unclassified assets default to High / High / High. TSW applies conservative H/H/H requirements and a multi-agency fail-safe to any asset without a profile, which can elevate PAIN and shorten deadlines. TSW flags these assets so you can correct them; results update when PAIN is recalculated.

06 / Keep coverage current

When to rerun
the skills.

There is no fixed schedule; assignments are generally stable. Review coverage when you add a resource or when an asset’s role, data, impact, or agency scope changes.

Already covered? The rule applies.

Cloud rules can match tags or names, including regex. Kubernetes rules can cover workloads by name or namespace. New assets that match receive the existing profile—confirm it actually fits their role.

A gap or changed role? Update the assignment.

Edit the metadata or run update-k8s-vdr-configmap / update-cloud-vdr-config. TSW uses the new profile when PAIN is recalculated.

FAQ / Common questions

Frequently asked
questions.

Grouped by topic. For questions about your environment, contact your stackArmor engagement manager or use our shared Slack channel.

Transition & reporting

How does this impact my existing findings, POA&Ms, and due dates?
  • Baseline scanning doesn’t change. Keep using the same methods—Nessus vulnerability and database scans, web application scans—per FedRAMP and RA-5.
  • Finding scopes and due dates do change. TSW groups findings differently from the previous GitLab process and sets due dates from PAIN, exploitation likelihood (LEV), and internet reachability (IRV). FedRAMP VDR findings move from GitLab to TSW. DoD/DoW workloads keep the existing workflow.
  • It’s a one-time reset, managed as an adaptive change. TSW ingests current vulnerabilities and compliance findings, then assigns due dates under VDR rules.
  • Counts won’t match. Previous POA&M item counts won’t equal TSW finding counts because the scopes differ. We can show that previously tracked findings are still tracked and none were dropped.
  • Deviation Request forms go away. You manage findings through attestations in TSW (false positive or operational requirement). Findings under existing approved deviation requests need a new attestation during the reset. Accepted findings appear in the accepted-findings JSON report for agency review.
  • Reporting. TSW produces current, accepted, and historical exports in the FedRAMP JSON formats. On request, stackArmor also plans a CSV export in the familiar POA&M template, using the new scopes and due dates. Read the agency transition guide.
Will the existing vulnerability workflow remain available for DoD/DoW workloads?

Yes. If a system supports both DoD/DoW and FedRAMP workloads, you may need to run both the sponsor-required process and VDR / VER during the transition and possibly longer. Ask your sponsor whether it supports VDR-based evaluation or reporting and which existing requirements continue. Read the DoD/DoW guidance.

What should we discuss with our agency before the transition?

Ask how they are preparing for December 7, 2026, which reports they expect, and how they want changes in identifiers, impact ratings, and remediation dates explained. The planned CSV export does not mean a separate traditional POA&M process for FedCiv agencies. Use the agency coordination guide.

Should we classify a staging environment differently?

No. Make staging representative of production and use the same classification. TSW separates non-production accounts from production; their vulnerabilities show full PAIN scores but are excluded from FedRAMP requirement tracking, reports, and incident automation.

Running the skills

Which agent skills do we need to run?

generate-k8s-vdr-configmap for Kubernetes and generate-cloud-vdr-config for everything else in the cloud. Run both if you have both. See the platform table.

Do we have to use AI?

No, but it is highly recommended. You can prepare the metadata manually using the same format and review. Compare the two approaches.

Will the agent change our environment, and what data does it use?

Not on their own. The skills only use read-only commands, read system and asset metadata, and write local candidate files. They run with your own active CLI credentials, so the access you grant is the limit: use a read-only identity. They do not retrieve Secret values or agency data. Applying the ConfigMap or uploading the cloud YAML is a separate step you perform through change management. What the skills can access.

The skill fails to run. What should we check first?

The active CLI profile. Confirm kubectl, gcloud, AWS CLI, or Azure CLI is authenticated to the intended context, project, account, or subscription with the documented read-only permissions. Access preparation.

What if the validation output shows coverage gaps?

Run the Kubernetes or cloud update skill and supply the existing YAML when prompted. If the environment you scanned didn’t represent production, rerun against one that does. Coverage maintenance.

How often do we need to rerun the skills?

There is no fixed schedule—only when assets are added or their role changes. See when to rerun.

Scanning & platforms

Do we need to run the container scanner?

For Kubernetes, yes: production readiness requires the plugin deployed in-cluster and running against the ConfigMap with no default profiles. For non-Kubernetes cloud resources, no—you’re done once the YAML is uploaded and readable by TSW. Local and SDLC scans are optional for everyone.

Is the Kubernetes ConfigMap safe to deploy?

Yes. It is a metadata object the scanner reads; it doesn’t change application behavior, networking, or workload settings. Deploy it through your approved change-management process.

Is the HTML report the final production interface?

No. It is a local exploration tool; TSW provides the production experience. Treat both the HTML and JSON as security-sensitive.

Will VMs and managed databases still be supported?

Yes, fully. Their VDR scoring will be available in TSW. Because they use commercial scanning products, we can’t offer a local preview of their scores.

What about Docker Swarm, other container technologies, or our own scanner?

Docker Swarm isn’t covered by the skills yet, but we have a path forward. For other container technologies, contact us immediately. To keep your own container scanner, it must produce JSON in the identical Trivy VDR plugin format and deliver it to an agreed storage location—a substantial lift, so most teams adopt the stackArmor scanner for now. Our method

Did stackArmor evaluate other VDR solutions, such as Tenable or Wiz?

Yes. They offered capabilities our previous platform couldn’t, but incorporating them would have added significant customer cost, and none provided the complete VDR implementation we needed—particularly PAIN calculations using agency, workload, and affected-agency context. We also couldn’t confirm from public documentation how they handled protocols, trusted-source allowlists, attack direction, and discovery. Read the full rationale.

Will stackArmor integrate other vulnerability management technologies?

We are open to it. An integration needs a capability review and an explainable mapping into PAIN, LEV / NLEV, and IRV / NIRV across all eight factors, plus operational and cost fit. It would likely take substantial effort and could add customer cost. See the integration criteria.

How can we understand why TSW considers a vulnerability internet-reachable?

We look at the vulnerable component and its trigger alongside deployment evidence: exposed protocols, allowed sources, load-balancer behavior, inbound vs. outbound interaction, and whether another vulnerability supplies an entrypoint. Read the examples, including RADAR TRACE.
