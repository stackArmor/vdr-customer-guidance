Eight evaluation factors

How TSW meets the evaluation requirements.

TSW brings asset context, vulnerability intelligence, configuration detail, and conservative assumptions together to address the eight factors in FedRAMP VER-EVA-EFA. Each factor informs impact, exposure, exploitation likelihood, or the scope of response.

Evaluation factorHow our method addresses it
CriticalityAsset Security Impact Profiles capture independent confidentiality, integrity, and availability requirements (CR / IR / AR). TSW combines them with the vulnerability’s impacts, any agency intended-use ceiling, and affected-agency scope to calculate the Potential Agency Impact Number (PAIN).
ReachabilityAsset accessibility, the CVSS attack vector, and direct or confirmed indirect internet triggers determine whether a vulnerability is internet-reachable (IRV) or non-internet-reachable (NIRV). This considers payloads that reach vulnerable components through other services, including components without a public address. Reviewed environmental evidence can refine the determination.
ExploitabilityTSW evaluates exploitation likelihood using the Exploit Prediction Scoring System (EPSS), CISA Known Exploited Vulnerabilities (KEV) membership, and workload persistence. A lower EPSS threshold for IRV than for NIRV explicitly incorporates detectability into the exploitation-likelihood determination. KEV establishes a Likely Exploitable Vulnerability (LEV); ephemeral workload handling and CVSS chain prerequisites provide additional context.
DetectabilityTSW evaluates detectability through its exposure-based EPSS thresholds: the same EPSS score can produce a different LEV / NLEV determination for IRV and NIRV. This accounts for the greater opportunity to identify and probe internet-reachable software. The method conservatively assumes internet-reachable vulnerabilities can be discovered. How detectability informs likelihood.
PrevalenceTSW tracks vulnerability counts on each component and affected-component counts for each vulnerability or rule. Findings preserve the remediation component needed for a lasting fix, including each container or init-container reference and the scaling group that recreates VMs (AWS ASG, GCP MIG, or Azure VMSS). This identifies spread and the configuration locations still needing action.
PrivilegeAsset classification considers IAM roles, RBAC, service-account authority, credentials, impersonation, and workload privileges when assigning CR / IR / AR. This captures the consequences of compromising the asset’s authority. Chain analysis adds privilege prerequisites and projected capabilities; where evidence is unavailable, the method assumes privilege gain is possible and grants no reduction.
Proximate vulnerabilitiesCWE identifiers and CAPEC-derived capabilities and prerequisites identify chainable entrypoints. An IRV vulnerability may enable exploitation of a downstream NIRV vulnerability through the capabilities gained and the data flow. Supported chains can apply the IRV remediation timeframe downstream; environmental evidence and conservative assumptions remain distinguishable. How entrypoints affect downstream response.
Known threatsCISA KEV, EPSS enrichment, and vulnerability references inform threat evaluation and the likely / non-likely exploitation determination (LEV / NLEV). KEV records identify known exploitation and required action. When an applicable KEV due date precedes the calculated FedRAMP deadline, the earlier date governs.

Criticality, vulnerability impact, and agency scope determine PAIN. PAIN, Certification Class, exploitation likelihood, and internet reachability then select the remediation timeframe. Prevalence and the other contextual factors also guide investigation, grouping, and remediation scope; considering a factor does not require a separate numeric score or a PAIN increase.

The evaluation behind our choice

We evaluated other solutions, including Tenable and Wiz.

Those products offered capabilities our previous platform could not accommodate. In our evaluation, incorporating them into stackArmor’s service would have required passing additional costs to customers, which we expected to be significant.

When we began this work, none of the solutions we evaluated provided a complete out-of-the-box implementation of the VDR requirements for our service. We would still have needed to build custom evaluation and reporting around them. PAIN was a central example: the calculation needs agency and workload context, the asset’s security requirements, and whether compromise could affect one or multiple agencies.

We chose to build a method we could explain, maintain, and integrate into our service. Our review of third-party determinations also needed to establish how they accounted for protocols, source restrictions, attack direction, and discovery opportunities. The tools may already address these decision points; their treatment was not clear to us from the documentation available at the time of our evaluation. Products and capabilities continue to evolve. We remain open to integrating other solutions through the capability and evidence review below.

Accountability for the decision

A determination needs an explanation.

We need to understand the information and assumptions behind impact, internet reachability, and exploitability so we can own the outcome, explain it to an auditor, and guide a customer to the right corrective action. A vendor’s conclusion alone does not provide that explanation. Proprietary, closed-source analysis compounds the problem when its inputs, decision rules, and exceptions are unavailable for review.

In our view, this lack of transparency is the main reason agencies and FedRAMP have historically been wary of proprietary vulnerability evaluation systems and relied so heavily on raw CVSS scores, despite their limitations as measures of risk in a particular deployment. An inspectable scoring method gives reviewers a shared basis for understanding and challenging a result. Our approach carries that transparency into the environmental context needed for VDR.

During our evaluation, the public documentation available to us did not make clear whether—or precisely how—third-party tools evaluated the decision points below when reporting impact, reachability, and exploitability. The tools may account for these conditions. Without clear public documentation, however, we could not verify how those conditions changed a reported result, explain the determination to a customer or auditor, or confidently identify the right corrective action.

These are concrete questions we needed to answer about third-party results, and that our own method and future integrations must explain:

  • Impact: What data, trusted authority, or service could be affected, for which agencies?
  • Protocols and permitted sources: Does an exposure determination distinguish SSH from HTTPS, HTTP/2 from an HTTP/1.1 backend, and unrestricted internet access from an edge-enforced allowlist of trusted sources?
  • Attack direction: Does an AV:N finding require an attacker to connect inbound, or the vulnerable component to contact an attacker-controlled destination? Which evidence establishes that path?
  • Detectability and likelihood: How do exposure and opportunities to discover the affected software change the exploitability determination?
  • Corrective action: Which configuration change removes the affected component or prevents the required exploit path, and how will we verify it?

This is why we publish our methodology, classification guidance, scanner behavior, and research. Transparency helps a customer assess the decision and act on it.

What the vulnerable code actually receives

Exposure depends on the protocol and path.

A public IP address and an open port are starting facts. Evaluation also needs the exposed protocol, the portion of the component that can be reached, the permitted source addresses, intermediary behavior, and the conditions that trigger the flaw.

SituationWhat the evaluation must establish
HTTPS is public; the vulnerability affects SSH.The HTTPS listener does not by itself establish a route to the vulnerable SSH service. Check the affected service’s actual ingress and any indirect trigger.
An HTTP/2-only flaw exists behind a load balancer that forwards only HTTP/1.1.If the backend accepts traffic only from that load balancer, and no other route can deliver HTTP/2 to the affected listener, the HTTP/2 exploit path is blocked. Evaluate the load balancer’s own software separately.
A service accepts only a strictly curated set of public source IPs tied to trusted entities, with all other sources blocked at the edge.These verified restrictions can support reclassifying an asset or affected vulnerability from IRV to NIRV. Record the curated source addresses, their trusted ownership, and the edge enforcement that blocks connections from outside the list as the basis for the determination (for example, via internetReachable: "false" and internetReachableJustification in vdr-cloud.yaml or ingress class declarations in vdr-fedramp.yaml). This differs materially from unrestricted access by 0.0.0.0/0.

Each conclusion should identify the affected component, the applicable path, and the configuration evidence supporting it. For trusted-source allowlisting, that includes source ownership and the enforcement that excludes untrusted connections.

TSW already combines asset accessibility, attack vectors, confirmed indirect triggers, and reviewed environmental evidence. The more detailed protocol and direction analysis described next is being incorporated through RADAR TRACE.

Research being incorporated into TSW

AV:N can describe very different interactions.

A CVSS network attack vector (AV:N) does not identify who initiates the relevant interaction. A remote attacker might send a malicious request to a vulnerable listener. Alternatively, the vulnerable component might have to contact an attacker-controlled server and process its response. Both involve a network, but require different controls and evidence.

Attack profileRelevant question and response
Inbound listener
The attacker initiates the interaction with the vulnerable component.		Can the attacker deliver the required protocol or payload through the route? Evaluate ingress, listener configuration, protocol handling, and filtering.
Outbound vulnerable client
The component contacts an attacker-controlled destination.		What causes the request, which destinations can it reach, and what response triggers the flaw? Evaluate egress, destination control, redirects, proxies, and client configuration. Lack of public inbound access does not settle this question.

RADAR TRACE publishes evidence-backed CVE profiles describing the vulnerable role, interaction initiator, trigger, attacker requirements, and required path capabilities. Its example analyses include HTTP/2 Rapid Reset, a curl redirect credential leak, and Log4j callback behavior.

We are incorporating this work into TSW and have submitted the approach to NIST as a proposal for enriching the National Vulnerability Database (NVD). The public profiles describe generic CVE requirements; deployment evidence is still needed to decide whether a particular environment provides that path. The published reachability gates explain how to combine that evidence and preserve uncertainty.

Related factors, explicit assumptions

Detectability informs exploitation likelihood.

TSW explicitly incorporates detectability into exploitation likelihood by applying a lower EPSS threshold to internet-reachable vulnerabilities (IRV) than to non-internet-reachable vulnerabilities (NIRV). At the same EPSS score, reachability can therefore change the LEV / NLEV determination. This exposure-based threshold selection is a concrete way our method evaluates detectability. CISA KEV membership and workload persistence also inform exploitation likelihood.

The rationale is that internet-reachable software can be easier to identify and probe: responses may reveal versions or vulnerable feature behavior. Querying a public API alone may provide no way to identify the PostgreSQL version behind it. We account for that difference in discovery opportunity through the selected threshold.

Our method conservatively assumes vulnerabilities can be discovered if they are internet-reachable.

Proximate vulnerabilities

An entrypoint can change the downstream risk.

CWE identifiers and CAPEC-derived capabilities and prerequisites help identify when exploiting one weakness could enable another. An internet-reachable entrypoint may supply code execution, credentials, or input control that makes a downstream vulnerability exploitable along a data flow, even when the downstream component has no public listener.

We consider both the capabilities gained at the entrypoint and what the downstream flaw requires. Protocols, privileges, trust boundaries, data flows, and mitigation context determine whether the link is supported. A taxonomy relationship identifies potential chaining; environmental evidence establishes the particular path.

TSW’s supported capability chains on the same resource can give a downstream NIRV finding the IRV remediation timeframe without changing its reported direct exposure. This ensures the response accounts for the upstream entrypoint. Broader chaining and lateral-movement potential remain part of the conservative evaluation, while demonstrated paths and assumptions stay distinguishable.

Prevalence / Find every place the fix belongs

Track the remediation component.

A remediation component is the specific configuration component you must update so the fix survives a restart, redeployment, or scaling event. TSW tracks vulnerability counts on each component and affected-component counts for each vulnerability or rule, preserving the locations still needing action.

Container workloads: every image reference matters.

A service may contain several containers, sidecars, and init containers. Building a patched image is the first step; every affected reference in the workload configuration must use it. If you update only two of three locations, TSW continues to flag the third when it is observed in scanning.

Image referenceAfter the update
containers.apiPatched image deployed
containers.workerPatched image deployed
initContainers.migrateOld vulnerable image remains — update this reference.

Apply the correction in the owning workload definition and deployment source, such as Helm values or a GitOps manifest, so the next rollout preserves it.

Auto-scaling VMs: fix what creates the next instance.

For auto-scaling VMs, TSW tracks the scaling group as the remediation component: an AWS Auto Scaling Group (ASG), a GCP managed instance group (MIG), or an Azure Virtual Machine Scale Set (VMSS). Updating a running instance alone can leave the group configured to create vulnerable replacements. Update its launch template (AWS), instance template (GCP), or scale set model (Azure) and image reference, then refresh or replace affected instances. Verify both the existing instances and newly created replacements.

Prevalence describes how much of the offering is affected and where response is needed. It does not require a separate PAIN multiplier.

Open to other technologies

What another integration needs to provide.

We are open to evaluating other vulnerability solutions for TSW, including Tenable, Wiz, and other technologies. A proposed integration needs a full capability review and an explainable translation into PAIN, Likely Exploitable Vulnerability (LEV / NLEV), and Internet-Reachable Vulnerability (IRV / NIRV), addressing the eight evaluation factors.

  • Agency and asset context: the workload’s role, security requirements, privileged authority, and affected-agency scope needed for PAIN.
  • Reachability evidence: relevant routes, protocols, source restrictions, interaction direction, and exploit prerequisites, with clear treatment of unknowns.
  • Likelihood and threat evidence: the inputs and reasoning behind exploitability and detectability, including known exploitation and workload persistence.
  • Component and chain context: affected configuration locations, prevalence, proximate weaknesses, and mitigation or response state.
  • Reviewable outcomes: documented mappings, source evidence, assumptions, and validation sufficient to explain decisions and corrective action.
  • Operational and commercial fit: ingestion compatibility, ongoing maintenance, and a clear assessment of customer cost.

Contact your engagement manager or shared stackArmor Slack channel to propose a review. Support for a specific new product depends on that assessment. Additional integrations would likely require substantial time and engineering effort and could involve additional customer costs.

Existing supported scanner workflows continue. The engineering guide covers classification, metadata, scanning, and integration guidance across workload types. vdr-cloud.yaml captures metadata for non-container workloads such as VMs and managed databases, as well as cloud-native container workloads such as AWS ECS and GCP Cloud Run. Kubernetes workload metadata is supplied through the vdr-fedramp ConfigMap.

Public work and peer feedback

Transparency makes the method easier to challenge and improve.

We have sought feedback from third-party assessment organizations (3PAOs) and other cloud service providers through the CSP Advisory Board (CSP-AB). In those discussions, we have found broad alignment on the overall approach, with differences in thresholds, values, and asset labels.

Several participating CSPs have told us that our published work encouraged greater transparency in their own programs and that they adapted aspects of their methods to align with ours. We value that exchange and continue to invite scrutiny. These observations describe our discussions and feedback; we do not independently assess or speak for every provider’s implementation.

Our commitment is to make the inputs, reasoning, limitations, and customer actions clear enough for others to evaluate, reproduce, and improve.
